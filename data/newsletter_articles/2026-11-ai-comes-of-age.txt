## Editorial | The Battle is no Longer Just About Better AI
p: Imagine asking an AI assistant to find the best deal on groceries. It compares products, remembers your preferences, selects a basket and completes the purchase. You never visit a retailer’s website. For the customer, this is convenience. For the retailer, it raises a difficult question: who controls the shopping experience when an AI agent stands between the customer and the store? This difference in accessing AI is rising tension between Amazon and Walmart.
p: Meanwhile, Meta’s Muse and OpenAI’s Dots represent another shift: AI systems that remember context, work proactively and take action on behalf of their users but with a slight twist on who they target for taking proactive action.
p: Education on the other hand is grappling with the balance between personalisation and governance. Is letting students personalize their learning journey right? Or should universities govern their learning experience? What is the right way to teach?
p: These developments reveal a market exploring different ways to create value from AI. Some companies want to control the environment in which transactions happen; others want to build the agent that accompanies users across tasks and services.
p: There is no single right model. But the choices companies make now could shape how the AI ecosystem develops, which business models prove sustainable, and which specialised products emerge.
hl: AI is moving beyond a race to build the most capable model. It is becoming a contest to define what AI products should do, whom they should serve and how they should reach users.

## The Gatekeepers | Amazon blocks. Walmart opens the door.
img: https://drive.google.com/thumbnail?id=1SIhZcP8SdGmYZylGGFbjdbqm12cgfWTe&sz=w1600
p: Imagine a future in which you never open a retailer’s website. Instead, your AI agent compares prices across stores, checks product availability, selects the best option and places an order. The retailer still fulfils the purchase, but it may no longer own the customer's primary digital interaction. That possibility helps explain the growing tension between Amazon and Walmart.
p: On October 21, 2026, Walmart announced its Agent Gateway, built to let approved AI agents connect to its inventory and transact on behalf of customers. Its approach includes Agent Profiles, through which shoppers can authorise agents, distinguish their purchases from their own, and set approval requirements or spending limits. Walmart is attempting to accommodate agentic shopping while retaining rules over how agents access its services.
p: Amazon has taken a more restrictive approach, blocking third-party agents from shopping on its platform. Its position reflects concerns around authorisation, security and control of the customer experience. There is also a commercial consideration: if customers increasingly interact with Amazon through an external agent, Amazon could lose influence over product discovery, recommendations and the shopping journey.
p: The contrast invites a comparison with the technology industry's longstanding debate over open and closed ecosystems. Android has generally allowed a broader range of device makers and distribution choices than Apple's tightly integrated iOS ecosystem. Yet Walmart is not literally open-source, and Amazon is not simply closed-source. The relevant distinction is how much access each company grants external agents.
p: Neither approach is inherently right or wrong. Walmart could benefit from making its products accessible through more AI assistants; Amazon could prioritise tighter control over transactions, customer relationships and platform security.
hl: The strategic question is not whether a company is open or closed, but how much control it is willing to share and what it expects to gain or protect in return.

## The Agent Builders | Meta Muse and OpenAI Dots: Different visions of personal AI
img: https://drive.google.com/thumbnail?id=12akBwezUyq8_abZAmgWY91572iSd5kBy&sz=w1600
p: For much of the generative AI boom, the dominant experience was conversational: ask a question, receive an answer, and start another conversation when a new task arose. Personal AI agents change this relationship. They are designed to retain context, use connected tools and continue working towards goals beyond a single exchange. Meta’s Muse and OpenAI’s Dots illustrate this transition, but their product positioning suggests different routes into the market.
p: Meta presents Muse as a personal agent for everyday life. It can help with shopping, appointments, travel, email and longer-term personal goals. It runs in a dedicated cloud environment, can work through connected applications and is designed to keep making progress after the user leaves the app. Its integration with Meta's broader ecosystem, including its messaging experiences, reflects an ambition to make AI assistance feel like a natural extension of daily life.
p: On the other hand, OpenAI presents Dots as always-on agents for carrying work forward. A dot can use its own cloud computer, connect to applications and pursue projects such as research, analysis, content creation and software development. Its availability through ChatGPT and connected work platforms creates a path towards integrating persistent agents into professional workflows.
p: It is tempting to describe this as B2C versus B2B: Meta serving consumers and OpenAI serving businesses. But that distinction is too rigid. Both companies can serve personal and professional users. The more useful comparison is their product emphasis and distribution strategy: which tasks they prioritise, where users encounter the agent, and how the agent fits into existing habits.
p: This is also a contest over the relationship with the user. If an agent becomes the place where someone organises plans, delegates tasks and makes decisions, it could become a valuable gateway to many other services.
hl: The competition is shifting from who can build the most capable AI to who can make an agent indispensable in a particular part of a user's life or work.

## The Different Sides of AI in Education | Personalize or Authorize
img: https://drive.google.com/thumbnail?id=1ksI90Zc9vsZpQbDLPrVOmWFhmdI8Hs0t&sz=w1600
p: The choices emerging in commerce and personal AI have a direct parallel in education. Universities face a similar question: should AI be primarily an assistant chosen by each learner and educator, or a capability integrated and governed by the institution?
p: On one side is AI chosen by the individual. Students can use personal assistants to clarify difficult concepts, practise skills, explore career options and receive feedback tailored to their needs. Faculty can use AI to research topics, prepare learning materials, develop activities and support assessment. The appeal is flexibility: people can experiment with tools that suit their goals, pace and working style.
p: On the other side is institution-led AI. Universities can integrate AI into learning platforms, curriculum systems, assessment processes and student-support services. These systems can be designed around institutional learning outcomes, academic policies, data protection requirements and quality assurance. They may also make it easier to provide consistent support across programmes and cohorts.
p: Each approach has trade-offs. Individual choice encourages experimentation and personalisation, but may lead to fragmented experiences, uneven quality and inconsistent safeguards. Institution-led systems offer integration and oversight, but can become restrictive if governance prevents faculty and students from exploring useful new approaches.
p: These approaches need not compete. A university can provide institutionally supported AI tools while allowing appropriate individual experimentation. It can establish boundaries for privacy, academic integrity and assessment without prescribing every legitimate use of AI.
p: The important question is whether the technology improves learning, rather than simply making academic tasks faster. Students still need to develop their own reasoning, judgement and ability to work independently. Faculty need to retain responsibility for educational design and evaluation.
hl: The opportunity for universities is to combine the flexibility of personal AI with the accountability of institution-led systems. The aim should be to expand learning opportunities while protecting academic standards and learner development.

## No Single Winning Model | Different ways of creating value from AI
img: https://drive.google.com/thumbnail?id=1KTxWTWc4rAHhI5LjRA0OEh1O-cw6byga&sz=w1600
p: The first three stories point towards a common conclusion: there is no single winning model for AI. The market is developing through different approaches to access, product design and organisational responsibility.
p: Walmart and Amazon illustrate the choices facing platforms. One is creating a route for approved external agents to interact with its retail environment; the other is more restrictive about third-party agents. These strategies reflect different judgements about customer access, control and the value of owning the shopping experience.
p: Meta Muse and OpenAI Dots illustrate another dimension: how an AI product defines the problem it wants to solve. An agent focused on everyday activities and one designed around ongoing work may share underlying capabilities, yet create value through different experiences, integrations and workflows.
p: Education adds a third dimension. A student using a personal assistant and a university deploying an institutionally governed learning agent are not necessarily choosing between good and bad technology. They are addressing different needs and responsibilities. Personal flexibility matters, but so do privacy, academic integrity, consistent support and evidence of learning.
p: Across all three settings, the same pattern emerges. AI value is shaped not only by model capability, but also by distribution, access to relevant systems, trust, specialised workflows and the ability to fit into people's lives and organisations.
hl: The opportunity is no longer limited to building a powerful model. Value can also come from distribution, integration, customer relationships, specialised workflows and control over the point where decisions are made.

## Looking Ahead | From one AI race to many specialised markets
p: Technology markets often evolve from a period of broad experimentation into one where products, business models and areas of specialisation become more distinct. Early competition may centre on whether a new technology works at all. As it matures, the questions shift: who is it for, which problem does it solve best, how does it reach customers, and how does the business sustain itself?
p: AI appears to be entering this phase. The early generative AI race focused heavily on model capability: reasoning, coding, image generation and the ability to answer a wide range of questions. These capabilities remain important, but they are increasingly becoming ingredients in products rather than the complete product itself.
p: The next stage may be shaped by the choices visible in this month's stories. Amazon and Walmart are exploring different ways to manage the relationship between retailers and external agents. Meta and OpenAI are developing persistent agents with different product emphases and routes into users' lives and work. Education is busy optimising the mix between personalization and governance.
p: This growing differentiation and specialisation could lead to a wider range of AI products rather than a market dominated by a single approach. General-purpose models may continue to provide the foundation, while specialised agents and applications create value by addressing particular needs and workflows. The opportunity will increasingly depend on how effectively companies turn AI capability into useful, trusted and distinctive experiences.
p: There is no guaranteed winner in this transition. Greater openness can expand reach but reduce control. A tightly integrated ecosystem can provide consistency but limit choice. Broad agents can be convenient, while specialised ones may perform particular tasks more effectively. Companies will test these trade-offs, and customers will influence which approaches endure.
hl: AI's maturation may be marked not by one company or one model winning everything, but by the emergence of distinct products, business models and specialised markets built on a shared technological foundation. And the winning strategies could also be something that's as new and unheard of, further reinforcing the "New Technologies, New Opportunities" maxim. Only time will tell!
